Behind the story

by Maria Godoy, senior science and health editor and correspondent

People had warned me it might be tough to get Amish people to talk to me as a journalist — and probably impossible to get their permission to record their voice. Not a great omen for compelling radio storytelling.

Yet as I headed off to Lancaster County, Pa., last month to report on the biggest and deadliest measles outbreak in the U.S. in decades, I knew I wanted to hear directly from the community hit hardest.

So I turned to some good old shoe-leather reporting. My producer and I mapped out Amish-owned businesses in and around Lancaster County and spent a day driving around from shop to shop. At every stop, we introduced ourselves and explained the focus of our story. We asked Amish people (easily identifiable by their traditional dress) about their views on the measles outbreak and vaccination. While some declined to talk, others opened up about how measles had affected their families and communities, and why they had or hadn't decided to get vaccinated. To my surprise, one woman even let me record her voice.

Outside a farm supply store in a rural town, I met an Amish woman who was visibly pregnant. Measles can have devastating consequences during pregnancy. She told me her husband and two children had gotten vaccinated to protect her and her unborn child. She declined to give me her name at first, then got in a car driven by her hired non-Amish driver. As they exited the parking lot, the driver stopped to ask us what media outlet we were with. "Oh, NPR!" the driver said when we replied. Then she turned to her Amish passenger and told her, "Don't worry, you can trust them."

Part of gaining trust is about showing up, showing respect and really listening. Word gets around that you're trustworthy. Public health officials in Pennsylvania have also been using a similar approach to win the trust of Amish people as they seek to boost vaccination rates in the current outbreak. You can read more about it here.
